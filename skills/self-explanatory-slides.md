# Self-Explanatory Slides

Apply the internal "Self-Explanatory Slides" guidelines whenever the user asks you to build, draft, improve, tidy, or review a presentation deck / slides / pptx — including prepping decks for principals, management, or approval / discussion / information meetings. Work in one of two modes:

- **Create** — produce or draft a deck that follows the guidelines below.
- **Review** — audit an existing deck against the guidelines and flag gaps concretely.

## Purpose
Presentation decks are often read without the presenter speaking to them. Slides must stand on their own: clear punchlines, holistic context, and no reliance on verbal explanation. This is the style used by consultancy firms.

## Core principle
The challenge is not what *more* to add, but deciding what *must* be in the deck and what can be left out. Always prioritise the main message: what is important and why it is important.

## Storyboarding
- Consider the overall flow so the deck is easy to comprehend end-to-end.
- Identify the key audience and address their key concerns directly.
- Identify key priorities and the supporting information needed to back them.
- Consider a second section / deck titled **"Context for Today's Discussion"** with detailed background for reading only — also usable to reinforce points live.

## Organisation
- Open with a **content overview / roadmap** that sets the flow and narrative.
- Set the context for the issue up front.
- Prioritise and highlight key content on presentation slides; add supporting detail as needed.
- Prepare a **single deck for circulation**, tagging the top-right corner **"Presentation"** on slides meant to be shown live.
- Keep "Presentation"-tagged slides to **≤12 for approval / discussion** items and **≤7 for information** items.
- Close with a clear summary of what is for info / discussion / approval and the next steps, to focus principals' attention.
- Include a **"resting slide"** — the slide left on screen when the presentation ends, used to centre the discussion. It can be the final slide or an earlier one.

## Content
- Write out punchlines that would previously have been said aloud.
- Use the **slide title / header to deliver the key point or punchline**, not a generic label.
- Err on the side of being verbose in clarifying each slide's content.
- Fold in detailed explanation slides (previously sent to the Annex) so the reader need not flip back and forth. Reserve the Annex for factsheets.
- Use diagrams, charts, and infographics to make content self-explanatory; where a visual is hard to grasp, add text explaining the relationships and conclusions.
- Use colours and visual cues (tables, boxes) to highlight main points.

## General rules
- Spell out acronyms in full on first use.
- Minimum font size **12** for readability.
- Assume slides are taken as read; be ready for the audience to ask only "anything to add?" — the resting slide is the base to centre conversation around.

## Role of management / team leads (guidance when advising leads)
- Ensure the presentation flow is comprehensive.
- Guide the team not to crowd out the main message and issues.
- Ensure content prioritises the main message: what is important and why.

## Review checklist
When reviewing a deck, check and report on each item:
1. Roadmap / overview slide present at the start?
2. Context / issue set up front?
3. Titles carry the punchline (not generic labels)?
4. "Presentation" slides tagged top-right and within the cap (≤12 approval / discussion, ≤7 info)?
5. Closing summary of info / discussion / approval + next steps?
6. A clear resting slide?
7. Detailed explanations in-deck (not pushed to Annex); Annex only holds factsheets?
8. Diagrams / charts used, with explanatory text where needed?
9. Colours / tables / boxes highlight key points?
10. Acronyms spelled out on first use; font ≥12?

Report gaps concretely (e.g. "16 presentation slides — over the 12 cap"), and note what could be cut rather than only what to add.
